What Would an Agentic AI Quant Researcher Do?

One day, five AI models and me. Together we built a working lab for testing famous investing rules. This is how the work was split, how fast it went, and why the team kept catching its own mistakes.

6½hours from a one-page brief to a working lab
5AI models working side by side, checking each other
13famous investing strategies, tested on 16 years of prices
0passed, and that turned out to be the honest answer

Quantitative research is a fascinating field. People take an idea about markets, write it down as a precise rule and test it against years of historical prices, and most of the craft lies in not fooling yourself along the way. I know data and I know financial services. I am not a quant.

So I asked myself a question: what would an AI quant researcher actually do? Reading about it seemed slow, so I decided to staff one and watch.

The staff was a set of AI models, each running in its own terminal window and passing work to the others through a small tool I had built the week before. The brief was ambitious. In one day, build a research lab from an empty folder, then use it to test thirteen well-known investing strategies honestly. I expected the agents to be fast. I also expected them to be weak at maths.

Meet the team

The lead · Claude Opus

Wrote the plan, split it into tasks, wrote a short brief for each one, and checked and merged the results. It wrote little of the code.

The specialist · Codex

Got the hardest jobs and the toughest reviews. It also built a second, independent version of the lab's engine to check the first.

The workhorses · OpenCode models

Cheaper models that did the well-defined work until their weekly allowance ran out in the afternoon.

The relief crew · Haiku and Luna

Two small, inexpensive models that took over when the workhorses ran dry. One redesigned the website. The other made the slowest step about 50 times faster.

One house rule mattered more than any other. Nobody checked their own work. The important pieces went to a reviewer built by a different company, so Codex's code was read by a Claude model and the lead's plans were read by Codex.

A lab by lunchtime

I pasted in a one-page charter at 11:35. Six minutes later the lead had committed a plan. By noon, four agents were working at once on different parts of the lab. One built the engine that replays a strategy over history, one loaded the price data, one wrote the statistics, and one built the second engine.

At 12:50 the lab produced its first result. It produced it a second time with identical output, which is the first thing a research lab should be able to do. By mid-afternoon it had a library of thirteen strategies, had run each one against 73 funds and sixteen years of prices, and was showing the results on its own website. The last change to the lab itself landed at 18:09, six and a half hours after the charter.

The speed came from working side by side. While one agent built the website, another wrote the strategies and a third reviewed the morning's code. The lead kept everyone supplied with clear tasks and made sure two agents never edited the same file.

My job: decide, not do

I wrote no code and checked no maths. My day looked more like running a small team. I set the direction, made the calls the agents left to a human, unblocked tools that got stuck, and looked at the results.

A few of those calls changed the project. Around 1:45 pm, with the basic lab working, I raised the stakes. Stop testing one idea and test real, well-known strategies. Use far more data. Make the website something a person would want to read. When the first redesign came back looking plain, I said so. The lead mocked up three designs, I picked one, and it was live in under half an hour.

I also kept an eye on the budget. Each model comes with its own subscription, so the cheap ones went first. When the lead was using twice as much as Codex, I told it to hand more work over.

Checking each other's homework

This is the part that surprised me most. I had expected the agents to struggle with the maths. The maths was fine. The mistakes turned up in the instructions, and several were in instructions the lead itself had written. What caught them was the house rule. A different agent always looked.

  • The recipe that peeked at the future.The lead wrote a rule for one strategy that quietly used information from later years to make decisions in earlier ones. That is like betting on a race after reading tomorrow's paper. Codex was asked to build it, read the rule first, and flagged the problem before writing any code.
  • Two engines that had to agree.Codex built a second engine from the written description alone, without seeing the first. Writing it out exposed an error in how the lead had specified trading costs. Now the two engines must match to twelve decimal places, and they do.
  • An unfair comparison.Every strategy is compared with random lookalikes, to rule out luck. A reviewer from a different AI company noticed that one set of lookalikes held about half as much stock as the real strategy, so beating them proved nothing. It was fixed before any result existed.
  • The lab that said no to its boss.During the final batch of experiments, the lead slipped in an unrelated change. The lab refused to run the next experiment. Every result records exactly which version of the code produced it, and that record would have been wrong.

No single agent was reliable on its own. The team, with every piece checked by someone else, was.

I heard about each catch from the lead's reports as the day went on. Why trust results I could not check by hand? Because of the same two things every time: independent sessions checking each other, and repeated runs that land on the same result.

So, do the famous strategies work?

The thirteen strategies are the classics. Buy what went up last year. Buy the calmest funds. Own stocks only in winter. To pass, a strategy had to clear three hurdles, all written down before any data was touched. It had to beat a simple benchmark by a clear margin, beat a thousand random lookalikes, and still look good after a penalty for having tried thirteen ideas.

None passed. Below are all thirteen, with a mark for each hurdle. Use the buttons to lower the bar and watch what happens.

Relax the rules and "winners" start to appear. That is why the bar was fixed in advance. For twelve strategies the honest answer is "the data can't tell". One, buying last month's biggest losers, did clearly worse than the benchmark. The closest call was a strategy that owns less stock when markets get jumpy, and it is the obvious one to look at again.

"Nothing passed" is less exciting than a winner. It is also what a careful lab should usually report. Finding a money machine in one afternoon would have been the real red flag.

What an AI quant researcher does

My answer, after the day:

"You can take a theory and translate it not only to technical implementation but also to heavily tested results, both horizontally and vertically validated. This all is done autonomously and much, much faster than the human output would be."

Look at where the day actually went and very little of it was maths. Most of it resembles good management.

  • Write instructions precise enough to check.When two agents build the same thing separately and get the same answer, the instructions were clear.
  • Decide what counts as a win before looking.The pass mark was set before the first result, so nobody could move it.
  • Never mark your own homework.The important work went to a reviewer from a different model family.
  • Write everything down.Every brief, review and result is in the project history, so any number can be traced to where it came from.

The human part did not go away. It moved. I chose what to ask, set the level of ambition, judged the design, and decided when something was good enough. The agents did the rest, in parallel, checking each other as they went.

A few honest limits. This was one day, one person and one project. It says nothing about whether a team of agents beats a single agent, because that comparison was not run. Nobody timed the human hours or added up the cost. The strategy results apply to these funds, these years and these exact rules, not to the strategies in general.
